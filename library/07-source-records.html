Annotated source records

Research snapshot: 12 November 2026. Source access describes the material actually inspected, not an assertion that unavailable material is absent. Counts refer to the units named.

Evidence records

S01 — Learning performance and unassisted assessment

Source: Hamsa Bastani, Osbert Bastani, Alp Sungu, Haosen Ge, Özge Kabakcı and Rei Mariman, “Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics,” PNAS 122(26), e2422633122, 25 June 2025, doi:10.1073/pnas.2422633122. The 20 August 2025 correction was inspected: it corrects Osbert Bastani’s affiliation after a production error; it reports no changes to findings.

Access and design: Publisher article and the relevant sections of the author-hosted manuscript and appendix inspected. The publisher-hosted supplement returned HTTP 403; equivalence to the author-hosted version was not independently established. The preregistration itself was not inspected. Preregistered classroom-assigned field experiment at one Turkish high school in fall 2023, grades 9–11. Four 90-minute sessions; approximately 50 classes and nearly 1,000 real students, not model-generated participants. The author-hosted appendix Table 3 reports 839 students in the main survey sample: 277 GPT Tutor, 320 control, and 242 GPT Base; its survey sample including honors contains 943 students. The author version says the main sample excludes honors classes and nonrespondents to the baseline survey. These counts should not be described as total enrollment. The publisher main Table 1 reports 2,848 student-session observations per outcome, not 2,848 people. Exact enrollment and class counts remain unverified. Unit of assignment: classroom; student-session performance analyzed, with classroom-clustered standard errors. Honors classes excluded from main sample. Students worked from teacher-provided problems, with notes/textbooks alone, a GPT-4 chat interface, or a teacher-informed GPT-4 tutor including solutions and hints, then took an unassisted exam.

Finding: Relative to controls, practice scores were higher with both AI variants; the standard interface group scored 17% lower on the subsequent unassisted exam relative to controls. The guarded tutor group did not show a positive exam effect relative to controls, and the negative effect was largely mitigated. These are short-term results for this setting and these specific tools; they do not establish long-term outcomes, effects on all ages, or that every guardrail works. Mechanism analyses suggest copying or reduced engagement, but do not definitively isolate the mechanism. See publisher methods and results.

S02 — Student AI competencies

Source: Fengchun Miao, Kelly Shiohira and Natalie Lao, UNESCO, AI competency framework for students (2024), official publication page; full framework, DOI 10.54675/JKJB9835.

Access and design: Official overview and parts of framework PDF inspected. Normative curriculum framework, not a trial or a participant sample. Twelve competencies span human-centred mindset, ethics, AI techniques and applications, and system design, across understand, apply and create. It recommends agency and critical judgment. This specifies educational goals; it does not show that any particular curriculum causes improved learning.

S03 — Educator competencies

Source: UNESCO, AI competency framework for teachers (2024), official framework PDF.

Access and design: Selected PDF sections inspected; normative professional-development framework, not a controlled outcome study. It emphasizes teacher agency, privacy, critical review, human interaction and learner control. Its recommendation to test pedagogical strategies is a program-design principle, not proof of efficacy.

S04 — Age-appropriate implementation

Source: UNESCO, Guidance for generative AI in education and research, 7 September 2023, official overview.

Access and design: Official overview inspected; full guidance not fully reviewed. Policy and pedagogical guidance, not a trial. It calls for privacy protections, age limits for independent conversations, and age-appropriate validation and design. Specific age thresholds and current jurisdiction-specific legal requirements have not been verified here; avoid presenting any recommendation as a universal law.

S05 — Children's rights and safeguards

Source: UNICEF Innocenti, Guidance on AI and children, version 3.0, December 2025, official report page.

Access and design: Issuer's overview inspected; complete PDF not reviewed. Rights-based guidance informed by consultation and existing evidence, including a twelve-country study; that number is countries, not a participant count. The overview lists safety, privacy, fairness, transparency, accountability, development, inclusion and children's AI skills among ten requirements. These are recommendations, not evidence that a particular safeguard has been validated or legal advice.

S06 — Conversational AI and children

Source: UNICEF, When AI becomes a friend: Child rights risks, harms, and regulatory responses to AI chatbots and companions, June 2026, official document page.

Access and design: Issuer overview inspected, not full policy brief. Policy analysis compares approaches across six jurisdictions as of May 2026; six is a jurisdiction count, not children or experiments. It identifies risks and recommends preventive safeguards and accountability. The overview does not quantify risk or establish prevalence.

S07 — A short literacy intervention

Source: Brett Puppart and Jaan Aru, A Brief AI Literacy Intervention Does Not Significantly Reduce Over-Reliance and Increases Under-Reliance on ChatGPT: A Randomized Study, arXiv:2503.10556v2, revised 18 September 2026; originally submitted 13 March 2025 under a different title. DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2503.10556.

Access and design: Selected methods, results and limitations of the 28-page v2 PDF inspected, as well as the version history. Preprint; peer-review or subsequent publication status not established. In October 2024 researchers invited 72 Estonian schools; 10 forwarded invitations. Forty-two real 12th-grade students completed the questionnaire; six were excluded for stated speed criteria, leaving 36 analyzed, ages 18–19: 19 intervention and 17 control. Individuals were randomized. Each saw eight selected math puzzles and fixed screenshots of model recommendations, four correct and four incorrect, and decided whether to adopt them; they did not interact live with the model. The control read a general introduction and the intervention received additional information. Participant and trial are distinct units.

Finding and limitations: Across participants, the reported mean proportion of incorrect recommendations adopted was 52.1%. The intervention group’s mean was 48.68% versus 55.88% for controls; the difference was not statistically significant (p = .471), which does not establish equivalence or no effect. Rejection of correct advice was higher in the intervention group (36.84% versus 19.12%; p = .025). The small sample, selection, exclusion criteria, fixed screenshots and short task constrain precision and generalization; the study does not establish that AI literacy education generally fails, or what happens in ordinary iterative chatbot use.

S08 — Undergraduate physics tutor trial

Source: Greg Kestin, Kelly Miller, Anna Klales, Timothy Milbourne and Gregorio Ponti, “AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: an RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting,” Scientific Reports 15, 17458, 3 June 2025. doi:10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6.

Access and design: Publisher article methods and results inspected; supplement not inspected. Two-week crossover randomized experiment in one Harvard undergraduate introductory physics course, fall 2023. Of 233 enrolled students, 194 met inclusion criteria involving consent, participation in both modes and completed tests. Peer-instruction groups of 2–3 were assigned together. The study compared an AI tutor used at home with in-class active learning on two topics; its tutor used pre-written answers and content-specific prompting. The reported post-test counts are 142 AI-condition and 174 classroom-condition lesson observations, not distinct people.

Finding and limits: The paper reports higher immediate post-test performance in the tutor condition. The environments, delivery and conditions differ together; this does not isolate AI from all other differences, measure long-term retention, establish effects in other learners or show that AI generally replaces teachers.

S09 — Nigeria after-school pilot

Source: Martin Elias De Simone, Federico Hernan Tiberti, Maria Barron Rodriguez, Federico Manolio, Wuraola Mosuro and Eliot Jolomi Dikoru, From Chalkboards to Chatbots: Evaluating the Impact of Generative AI on Learning Outcomes in Nigeria, World Bank Education Global Department working paper, May 2025. Working paper PDF; reproducibility package DOI.

Access and design: Working paper methods and results and reproducibility-package overview inspected; data and code not independently rerun. Interested first-year senior secondary students in Benin City, Nigeria, were individually randomized: 657 to a six-week teacher-supported after-school AI program and 671 to usual schooling. The endline assessment included 422 treatment and 337 control students, 759 total; these are people, not sessions. The program combined materials, adult support and GPT-4-based Copilot. The control did not receive an equal-time alternative.

Finding and limits: The authors report a 0.31 standard-deviation improvement in a combined English, AI-knowledge and digital-skills assessment and 0.238 standard deviations in English; the relevant regression reports 654 observations, fewer than the 759 endline completers. Differential attrition was significant; the paper reports sensitivity analyses, but selection remains a limitation. The bundled intervention does not establish the separate effect of AI, nor its long-term impact. The working paper’s projected earnings are projections, not observed participant outcomes.

S10 — Medical education observational study

Source: Thomas Thesen and Soo Hwan Park, “A generative AI teaching assistant for personalized learning in medical education,” npj Digital Medicine 8, 627, 4 November 2025. doi:10.1038/s41746-025-02022-1.

Access and design: Publisher methods, selected results and limitations inspected. Observational deployment of a course-material-grounded assistant across two medical-school cohorts, not a randomized learning-outcome trial. Among survey respondents, 22 of 56 in the first cohort and 23 of 87 in the second reported at least one use. The paper reports 328 conversations in thematic analysis (216 / 65.9% in its first topic category); conversations are not distinct participants, and topic codes can overlap. GPT-4o assisted thematic coding; a 15% subset received human validation. The authors did not systematically assess answer accuracy or track individual engagement patterns. Findings describe usage and reported experience in this setting; they do not establish learning gains or that retrieval eliminates hallucination.

S11 — Evaluating education-technology privacy

Source: U.S. Department of Education, Student Privacy Policy Office, Privacy and Education Technology, webpage, publication date not established; accessed 12 November 2026.

Access and design: Agency overview inspected, linked model terms and underlying legal guidance not individually reviewed. The page links resources on assessing how online services collect, use and transmit information. This is a US agency resource, not a study or a complete statement of requirements in every jurisdiction. Laws and interpretations can change.

S12 — Child privacy and schools

Source: U.S. Federal Trade Commission, Complying with COPPA: Frequently Asked Questions, section N, webpage accessed 12 November 2026.

Access and design: Selected agency FAQ on schools inspected; not a trial. It describes limited circumstances in which a school may act for a parent in relation to educational services, and distinguishes school-benefit uses from other commercial purposes. The detailed requirements and applicable law depend on context and may change. Dux does not provide legal advice or assert that this US guidance applies elsewhere.

S13 — AI risk management framework

Source: Elham Tabassi, National Institute of Standards and Technology, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), 26 January 2023, doi:10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1; core functions.

Access and design: NIST publication overview and online core inspected. Voluntary, non-sector-specific governance framework, not a school efficacy study or a binding universal standard. It organizes activities around govern, map, measure and manage, and says the functions are not a checklist. NIST notes a revision is in progress; check current status before use.

S14 — Web accessibility guidance

Source: World Wide Web Consortium, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2, W3C Recommendation, 12 December 2024.

Access and design: Recommendation overview and publication history inspected. Technical accessibility standard, not an empirical evaluation of an AI tutor. W3C states its guidelines do not address every need. Conformance of any Dux or third-party service has not been established by inclusion here.

S15 — Education technology in context

Source: UNESCO Global Education Monitoring Report Team, Technology in education: A tool on whose terms?, 2023, official publication page.

Access and design: Issuer’s overview and selected summary text inspected, not every underlying study. The report considers education technology through relevance, equity, scalability and sustainability. Its broader education-technology scope must not be recast as a trial of generative AI. It advocates learning centred on people and attention to potential adverse consequences.

S16 — Teacher AI program in Peru

Source: Carla Z. Glave, Carolina Lopez, Ezequiel Molina and Román Andrés Zárate, Not Faster, but Better: Generative AI and Teacher Skill in Peru 2025, World Bank dataset description, 20 July 2026, doi:10.48529/t6x6-2y70.

Access and design: Primary data catalogue description and abstract inspected; full paper, dataset and preregistration not inspected. The record describes 1,269 sixth-grade teachers across 390 public schools in metropolitan Lima, with school-network eligibility and school-level randomization. It identifies a program combining training, a Copilot licence and follow-up, with further randomized message features. The catalogue abstract reports greater adoption without measured time savings on core tasks; this is a bundled program claim, not evidence on student learning or on AI software in isolation. The catalogue is a research lead: outcome measures, attrition, uncertainty and paper version require full-paper verification before stronger claims.

S17 — Undergraduate preparation and belonging

Source: Renlin Wang, Jian Sun and Shu Wen, “Structured generative AI preparation and classroom belonging in a six-class randomized trial,” Scientific Reports, accepted article published online 26 October 2026, doi:10.1038/s41598-026-78039-7.

Access and design: Publisher abstract inspected; complete methods, data and supplement not independently reviewed. The publisher labels it an accepted version subject to further editing. Six intact classes at one Chinese university were randomized, three sections and 110 students per condition. The intervention combines AI exploration, confidence reflection, question generation and instructor responsiveness. It measures classroom belonging and related reported outcomes; the section is the assignment unit, and the number of sections is six, not 220 independent randomized units.

Finding and limits: The abstract reports a difference in belonging at week 12 under its section-level analysis, while its exact two-sided randomization tests give p = .100 for the primary contrasts. The complex intervention does not isolate AI or establish effects in other settings. This is a limited-access research lead pending full methods review, not a broad claim that AI improves belonging.
