Implementation: access is not the same as use

Children · 2 min read

On this page

  1. Two elementary-school trials
  2. Ask what happened in practice
  3. Avoid a simple dose story

An education system may make a tool available without learners being able or willing to use it as intended. Time, access, relationships, technical reliability, privacy, motivation and the fit between activity and learner can all affect implementation. Usage is an outcome to examine, not proof that learning occurred.

Two elementary-school trials #

A 2026 working paper reports two randomized trials in which children used the same AI reading platform independently or with an in-person tutor supporting engagement. Across the districts, 355 children were assigned; the programs differed in setting and tutor type. The human-support group spent slightly more time on the platform and read more stories, but overall usage remained low. Exploratory reading scores did not significantly improve. The comparison does not tell us whether AI reading instruction beats a human tutor or no intervention. S22

Ask what happened in practice #

A pilot can document the number offered access, who actually takes part, how often, what support is needed, and why people decline or stop. Separate these observations from whether learning improved. Participation data can themselves be sensitive, so collect only what is necessary and make reporting transparent. These are evaluation proposals, not measured outcomes of a Dux pilot. S11 S13

Avoid a simple dose story #

The provider of the studied platform offered a recommended usage level. That statement is a provider claim; the two trials do not establish a universal safe or effective number of minutes. Increased screen time may displace another useful activity. Compare the full learning experience, including human relationships and accessible alternatives, before deciding whether more use is desirable. S22
