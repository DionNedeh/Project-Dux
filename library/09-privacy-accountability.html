Privacy, data and accountability

Educational tools may handle prompts, student work, usage logs, account information and sensitive context. Institutions should understand the actual service and its data flows before adopting it. A model answer can also contain invented personal or factual information. Adult oversight does not by itself erase these risks. S04 S05

Questions to ask a provider

  • What information is necessary for the learning activity, and what can be omitted?
  • Who can access it, where is it stored, and how long is it retained?
  • Can it be used for advertising, training or other purposes? What choices actually exist?
  • How can a person correct or remove information, report an incident, or leave the service?
  • What happens if the service changes its terms or is unavailable?

These are due-diligence questions, not proof that any answer is sufficient. The US Department of Education links a model terms checklist for evaluating collection, use and transmission. Its page is a US resource and does not settle legal requirements for every organization. S11

Children and school agreements

The FTC’s school FAQ describes specific conditions for school consent in the US and distinguishes school-benefit uses from other commercial purposes. Whether and how an institution may use a service depends on current law, age, service design and agreements. Seek appropriate local advice; do not treat this overview as a legal checklist. S12

Human responsibility

Establish who approves a tool, who reviews its output, how learners can contest a decision, and how to suspend a use that is causing harm. Human review should have the time, authority and information to change an outcome. These are proposed governance practices grounded in institutional guidance, not verified results of a particular program. S03 S05
