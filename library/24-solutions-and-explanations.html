Solutions, explanations and hints

Independent learning · 3 min read

On this page

  1. With help and without it
  2. Asking for answers or for understanding
  3. When a worked example helps
  4. What this suggests, and what it does not

Several studies now compare learners who could ask an AI system for help with learners who could not, and then test them afterwards. Read together, they suggest that the kind of help matters at least as much as whether help was available. The pattern is consistent enough to plan around, but most of the evidence for it is short-term, and part of it is exploratory.

With help and without it #

Four studies measured performance with support separately from performance afterwards, without it. In a Turkish high school, open GPT-4 access raised practice scores but was followed by lower scores on an unassisted exam; a tutor built around teacher-provided hints largely avoided the drop. S01 In a lab study of English-as-a-second-language university students, ChatGPT support improved essay revisions more than a human expert, a checklist or no support, but knowledge gain, transfer and motivation did not differ significantly. S26 In two lab experiments with novice programmers, chatbot access let students cover more practice questions without a significant change in their later post-test. S23 A product made with support is a different outcome from what a learner can do alone; studies, and classrooms, should measure both.

Asking for answers or for understanding #

In the programming experiments, the researchers coded what students asked the chatbot. Requests for explanations were associated with better post-test results at a given level of progress. When copy and paste was enabled, students asked for more complete solutions, and, at the same level of practice progress, those with access solved fewer post-test questions than their counterparts in the earlier study without copy and paste. These analyses were exploratory: the explanation requests were not randomized, and the copy-and-paste comparison spans two separate studies. S23 They fit the Turkish result, where the guarded tutor steered students toward hints. S01

When a worked example helps #

The picture is not simply “solutions are bad”. In an online experiment with 274 adult crowdworkers, worked-solution hints written by ChatGPT and checked by a person before use produced immediate gains on a short algebra or statistics test; the gains with human-authored hints were not significantly different. Learners attempted each problem before seeing a hint, the test came minutes later, and about a third of generated hints had failed the human check and were replaced. S27 A purpose-built physics tutor with pre-written answers also produced strong immediate results. S08 Worked examples after an attempt, checked for accuracy, are a different kind of help from a chatbot that completes the exercise.

What this suggests, and what it does not #

For a learner: make an attempt first, ask for an explanation or a hint rather than the finished answer, and then try a similar problem without help. For an educator: decide which steps the tool may help with, and assess some learning without it. These are reasonable inferences from short studies, mostly with older students and adults. They are not proof that any one design works everywhere, and none of these studies measured long-term learning.
