AI literacy

AI literacy includes the ability to ask what a system is for, how its output can be checked, whose interests it serves, and who remains accountable. It is broader than prompt writing. UNESCO’s student framework describes 12 competencies across human-centred mindset, ethics, techniques and applications, and system design, at understand, apply and create levels. It is a framework for curriculum design, not a measured effect of a lesson. S02

Understanding and questioning

An age-appropriate lesson might compare a generated answer with a reliable source, inspect a plausible error, or discuss how training data and design choices shape outputs. Learners can ask who created the system, what information is missing, whether an answer can be checked, and when the task should remain human-led. These are Dux teaching proposals; their effectiveness needs evaluation.

Appropriate reliance

A small randomized Estonian study asked 36 students aged 18–19 to judge fixed screenshots of answers to eight puzzles. A short educational text did not significantly reduce acceptance of incorrect recommendations, and the intervention group rejected more correct recommendations. The experiment did not involve live prompting; its sample and artificial task limit generalization. A non-significant result is not proof of no effect. S07

This suggests testing what learners actually do, not only asking whether they remember a warning. Compare independently checked work, explanations of reasoning and the ability to identify uncertainty. A learner should be able to accept a justified answer as well as reject an erroneous one. This is a Dux inference, not a proven curriculum.

Beyond one age group

School frameworks need adaptation for adults, language learners, disabled learners, and people using assistive technology. The sources here do not establish which sequence or classroom activity works best for each population. Accessibility and choice should be designed with the people affected, then evaluated rather than assumed.
