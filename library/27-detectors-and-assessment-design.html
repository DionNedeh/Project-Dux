Detection, trust and assessment design

Assessment · 3 min read

On this page

  1. What detectors got wrong
  2. Designing for what a task should show
  3. What students say
  4. Practical implications

When students can produce fluent text with AI, it is tempting to look for a tool that can tell. The evidence collected here points the other way: detection scores are an unsafe basis for decisions about individual students, and the more durable response is to design assessments around what each task is meant to show.

What detectors got wrong #

In a 2023 benchmarking study, seven commercial and open detectors were run on 91 essays written for an English proficiency test by non-native speakers and 88 essays by US eighth-graders. On average the detectors labelled 61% of the non-native essays as AI-generated; at least one detector flagged 89 of the 91. The eighth-grade essays were classified almost perfectly. Asking ChatGPT to make the non-native essays sound more “native” cut the false-positive rate to about 12%, and asking it to simplify the eighth-grade essays raised theirs to about 57%. S29 Detectors have changed since early 2023 and the samples were small, but the mechanism the authors identify, that plainer vocabulary looks “machine-like” to these tools, would tend to fall hardest on language learners and on writers who use simpler phrasing.

Designing for what a task should show #

The AI Assessment Scale describes levels of permitted AI use, from none to full, so that an educator can match permitted use to the learning outcome and tell students clearly. Its 2025 revision moves further away from policing: it says tasks meant to be done without AI should happen in supervised settings rather than depending on detectors or honour codes, and that relabelling an unchanged task does not make it valid. S30 The scale is a design and communication framework; its developers note that its effect on learning or misconduct has not yet been established.

What students say #

In a 2025 Pew survey, 59% of US teens said students at their school use AI chatbots to cheat at least somewhat often. That is a perception of peers, not a measured rate. S32 The same teens drew distinctions: in a 2024 Pew survey, 54% thought using ChatGPT to research a new topic was acceptable, while only 18% said the same of writing an essay. S31

Practical implications #

  • Do not use a detector score as the sole basis for an accusation. Talk with the student and look at the work’s history.
  • Say clearly, task by task, what kind of AI use is allowed and why.
  • When a skill must be shown without AI, assess it in a setting where that can be seen, with accessible alternatives.
  • Where AI is allowed, ask learners to show their process: drafts, choices and what they checked.

These follow from the sources and from the library’s chapter on assessment in an AI-rich setting. None of the sources shows that a particular redesign reduces misconduct or improves learning, and rules on AI in assessment differ between institutions.
