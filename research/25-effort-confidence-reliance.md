# Effort, confidence and reliance

Generative AI can make a task feel easier. Whether that lighter effort also means less learning or weaker judgment is one of the most discussed questions in this field, and one of the least settled. The sources here mostly measure self-reports, short tasks or brain activity, not long-term learning.

## Feeling that one has learned

In two programming experiments, students with chatbot access rated their own learning higher than their measured post-test results justified. [S23](07-source-records.md#s23) In the Turkish mathematics study, students’ perceptions also did not match their exam outcomes. [S01](07-source-records.md#s01) Satisfaction and confidence are worth asking about, but they are not evidence that learning happened.

## Confidence in the tool and in oneself

A survey of 319 knowledge workers who used generative AI weekly described 936 workplace tasks. Participants reported less effort for most thinking activities when using AI. Higher confidence in the AI for a task was associated with less reported critical thinking; higher self-confidence went with more. The study is a one-time self-report survey of adults, so these are associations, not effects, and the authors note that some participants confused less effort using the tool with less thinking. [S25](07-source-records.md#s25) A small Estonian study with 36 analysed students found that a short explanation of AI’s limits did not significantly reduce acceptance of wrong advice, and increased rejection of correct advice. [S07](07-source-records.md#s07) Calibrated trust, not maximum suspicion, is the goal.

## Writing, memory and ownership

A widely shared MIT Media Lab preprint recorded EEG while 54 adults wrote short essays with ChatGPT, with web search or with no tool. The ChatGPT group showed the weakest connectivity, and most of its members could not quote from the essay they had just written; they also reported lower ownership. The paper is under review, the sample is small, its optional fourth session involved 18 self-selected participants, and a published commentary questions its methods. EEG connectivity is not a measure of learning. [S24](07-source-records.md#s24) At most it supports a narrow point: in one short task, adults who handed the writing to a chatbot remembered less of what “they” wrote.

## Reading these claims

Headlines about AI “rotting” or “boosting” the brain usually go further than the studies. Ask: Who took part? Was anyone randomized? Was learning measured, or a proxy such as effort, confidence or brain activity? Was it measured later, without the tool? See [reading claims carefully](22-reading-studies.md). None of the studies here involves children, and none follows learners for more than a few months.
