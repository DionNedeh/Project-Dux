# Learning activities to try and evaluate

These activities are examples, not validated lesson plans. Adapt them to learner age, disability, language, goals, resources and local policies. Participation in an AI activity should not be the only route to the learning objective.

## Compare, check, explain

Give a learner two short explanations of a topic, one generated and one from a suitable human-authored source. Ask what is supported, what is missing, how they would check a claim and what remains uncertain. Use examples appropriate to the learner; provide sources and human help. The aim is calibrated evaluation, not automatic distrust. The small short-text study shows that explaining limitations alone did not significantly change over-reliance on its task. [S07](07-source-records.md#s07)

## Try, seek a hint, return

Invite an initial attempt, then a hint or conversation with a teacher, peer, book or tool. Ask the learner to revisit the original problem and explain the decision. Offer needed assistive supports. A later, accessible transfer task can help evaluate whether the activity supported learning. The Turkish study motivates testing scaffold design, but does not prove this activity works. [S01](07-source-records.md#s01)

## Design an AI-free path

Invite learners to choose a period for reading, making, discussing or practicing without a generative tool when that fits the objective. Reflect afterward on what was easy, difficult or worth checking. This is an option, not a moral rule or an instruction to abandon accessibility support. Evidence in this collection does not establish a universal time threshold.

## For educators and families

Use a fictitious example to discuss personal information, consent and the option to stop; identify a trusted person to ask for help. Adults can review the tool and its rules in advance. These are safety-oriented suggestions informed by child-rights guidance, not evidence of their standalone effectiveness. [S05](07-source-records.md#s05)
