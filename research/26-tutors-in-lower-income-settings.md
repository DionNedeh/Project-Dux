# AI tutoring in lower-income settings

Some of the most encouraging results for AI in education come from programmes in low- and middle-income countries, where classes are large and extra tutoring is scarce. They deserve attention, and careful reading. In each case AI arrived as part of a supervised programme, not as an unsupervised app.

## Ghana: a maths tutor over WhatsApp

In schools run by one network in Ghana, students used a maths chatbot on school-provided phones during two 30-minute study-hall sessions a week, with teachers present, for about eight months. Eleven schools were randomized, five to the tutor. Students who completed both tests grew 3.01 points more on a 35-item numeracy test than controls. The control group had no equal-time alternative, the small number of schools and student-level analysis may understate uncertainty, an author is affiliated with the organisation that built the tool, and two versions of the paper report different samples. [S28](07-source-records.md#s28)

## Nigeria: an after-school programme

In Benin City, interested senior secondary students were randomized to a six-week, teacher-supported after-school programme that combined materials, adults and GPT-4. The working paper reports gains in English and a combined assessment; attrition differed between groups, and the programme was compared with no extra sessions. [S09](07-source-records.md#s09)

## Peru: support for teachers

In Lima, teachers offered training, an AI assistant licence and follow-up scored higher on prompting and judged weak AI output better, without measured time savings. Effects on students were not established. [S16](07-source-records.md#s16)

## What these studies share

Each bundled AI with structure: set times, supervision, curriculum-linked content or training. None isolates the AI component from extra practice time. Implementing organisations were involved in the research in at least two cases. Together they suggest that supervised, curriculum-bound use can help in some settings; they should not be added together as independent proof of large effects, or used to argue that devices alone will close learning gaps. The UNESCO GEM report’s questions about relevance, equity, scale and sustainability still apply. [S15](07-source-records.md#s15) Access to a device is not the same as use, and use is not the same as learning. See [access and actual use](23-access-and-use.md).
