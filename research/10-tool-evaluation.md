# Evaluating an educational AI tool

No single feature label proves a tool is safe, accurate or educationally effective. Evaluate a particular product, version and use case against a specific learning goal and a realistic alternative. A system designed to offer hints may still give answers or make mistakes. A system grounded in course documents can cite materials and still require independent accuracy checks. [S01](07-source-records.md#s01) [S10](07-source-records.md#s10)

## Before a pilot

Describe the learners, setting, expected benefits, accessibility needs, human supports, data handling and acceptable alternatives. Ask whether the same problem could be addressed by existing teaching, peers, books or an assistive tool. Define how learners and educators can decline, report problems or stop. These steps are Dux proposals to be adapted and evaluated.

## During a pilot

Observe whether the tool provides the help intended, whether learners can challenge or leave it, and whether errors reach students. Invite feedback without making participation coercive. Measure learning and accessibility as well as use and satisfaction. Monitor outcomes across groups only with appropriate privacy safeguards and sufficient sample sizes.

## After a pilot

Compare results with the stated goal and an appropriate alternative, including a later assessment where feasible. Consider the workload, cost, data risks and who was unable to participate. Report missing outcomes and uncertainty. The Nigerian intervention bundled AI access, teaching and extra time; its measured program effect cannot identify which component caused the result. The medical-school deployment observed usage and feedback, but did not systematically measure answer accuracy or establish improved learning. Its own authors note constraints on interpretation. [S10](07-source-records.md#s10)
