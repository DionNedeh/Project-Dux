# Accessibility and participation

Accessibility is part of the learning goal, not a final decoration. People may use screen readers, captions, alternative input, adjusted presentation, translated materials or assistive systems. The right combination depends on the individual and activity. A proposed AI tool should be assessed with disabled learners and the assistive technologies they actually use.

## Separate participation from a particular tool

Offer accessible ways to read, respond, ask questions and seek help. An AI-free option should not remove an accommodation someone needs; an AI-based option should not require a person to disclose disability or private information unnecessarily. These are Dux design proposals, not evidence of effectiveness in every setting.

## Standards and human review

W3C’s WCAG 2.2 provides recommendations for accessible web content and notes that it does not address every need. Automated tests can identify some issues but do not replace assessment with people and assistive technologies. A cited standard does not certify an application. [S14](07-source-records.md#s14)

## Equity beyond access

Ask who can afford the tool, whether connectivity is reliable, whether language and examples fit the learner, and whether participation exposes personal data. UNESCO’s broader education-technology report asks whether a technology is relevant, equitable, scalable and sustainable. Its assessment is not a test of a particular generative AI system.
