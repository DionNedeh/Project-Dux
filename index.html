AI & education

Learning, with intention.

AI is becoming part of how we learn. Project Dux explores how to use it thoughtfully—while keeping curiosity, judgment, and independent thinking at the heart of education.

The exploration / 01

Technology can support learning. People give it direction.

There is room for both possibility and caution. Dux is a place to consider what thoughtful use might look like for learners, educators, and families.

A thoughtful pause / 02

Before asking AI, ask yourself.

There is no single rule for every learner or classroom. These questions are starting points for a conversation, especially when children are involved.

What am I trying to learn for myself?
Could I try, think, or create first?
How will I check what the tool tells me?
Is this appropriate for the learner and the setting?

What kind of help fits this moment?

Qualitative guide

The same tool can help or get in the way, depending on what the moment is for. Start with the purpose, then choose the help.

Make the first attempt yourself. If you get stuck, ask for a hint or a question, not the answer.

Try this

  • Work the problem alone first, and mark where you got stuck.
  • Ask for one hint or one question, not a worked solution.
  • Then redo it without help, and explain each step out loud.

Keep in mind

  • Practice that feels easier is not the same as learning. In one high-school maths study, open chat help raised practice scores but was followed by lower scores on a later test taken without it.
  • Feeling confident is not the same as being able to do it alone.

Keep the thinking / 03

Help that keeps the thinking with you.

Eight requests a learner might type. Sort each one: does it keep the thinking with the learner, or hand it over? There is no score. Each card shows one way to see it, and some honestly depend on the purpose.

Keep it, or hand it over?

Qualitative guide

Card 1 of 8

“Write my essay about the water cycle.”

One way to see it

Not a test, and not a score

The same words can do different work. What matters is whether the learner still does the part that builds the skill: trying, choosing, checking, and explaining. Access support, like translation, can keep learning possible rather than replacing it.

    Learning activities to try →

    Some pairs to compare:

    • “Write my essay about the water cycle” hands the thinking over. “Ask me three questions that would make my essay stronger” keeps it with you.
    • “Give me the answer to question 4” skips the practice if you have not tried yet. “I got 12 for question 4. Where might my method have gone wrong?” keeps it.
    • “Summarise this chapter so I don’t have to read it” hands it over. “Quiz me on this chapter after I’ve read it” keeps it.
    • “Translate this article into my first language” depends on the purpose: often it is access support that makes the learning possible.
    People and tools / 04

    A tool can help. People stay responsible.

    Learners, educators, and families each hold parts of learning that a tool cannot. These two guides show who does what, and what to ask before a class or a family takes on a new AI tool.

    Who does what in a learning moment

    Qualitative guide

    These are general patterns, not rules about any one person, class, or tool. Ages, needs, and settings change them.

    Look at the part played by

    The learner does the part that builds the skill: trying, choosing, checking, and explaining. Others can support that work, but cannot do it for them.

    The learner’s part
    Set the goalCan helpSays what they want to understand or be able to do.
    Do the thinkingMain roleMakes the attempt, gets stuck, and works it through.
    Give feedbackCan helpReviews their own work and, sometimes, a peer’s.
    Judge what was learnedCan helpCan explain what they understand, and where they are unsure.
    Protect personal informationCan helpChooses what to type, with guidance suited to their age.

    Before taking on a new AI tool: step by step

    Qualitative guide

    For an educator, a school, or a family. Open any step. None of this is legal advice; rules differ by place and change over time.

    1. Name the learning goal

    What should the learner be able to understand or do afterward? If the tool would do that part for them, look for another way, or keep the tool for a different step.

    2. Ask who could be left out

    Think about cost, devices, connection, language, and disability. Participation in an AI activity should not be the only route to the learning goal, and accessibility support should not be taken away in the name of “doing it without AI”.

    Accessibility and participation →

    3. Read what happens to the data
    • What does it collect, and why?
    • Is anything used to train models or for advertising?
    • How long is it kept, and how is it deleted?
    • Who approved it, and is there an agreement?

    In the United States, the Department of Education and the FTC publish guidance for schools on these questions. Privacy, data and accountability →

    4. Check age rules and child safeguards

    Services set their own age terms and laws differ by country. Child-rights guidance raises particular concerns about chatbots that present themselves as friends. There is no universal safe age.

    Children and safeguards →

    5. Decide what stays human-led

    Which feedback, judgments, and conversations stay with a person? Who checks generated facts before they are taught?

    Teacher work, support and agency →

    6. Plan how you will know it helped

    Look beyond impressions and practice scores. A later task done without the tool, or an explanation in the learner’s own words, says more. Compare with the alternative you would otherwise use.

    Reading claims carefully →

    7. Plan how to stop, and how to report a problem

    Know who to tell if something goes wrong, what would make you pause, and how a learner can opt out without losing out. If a child is at risk of harm, contact the appropriate local authorities or emergency services.

    Governance across the life of a tool →

    People and tools

    Who is being supported?

    One tutoring study tested suggestions for human tutors, while another examined human support for children using a reading platform. Their comparisons are different.

    What is still unknown?

    Adults, children, educators and families have different goals and needs. The evidence here does not establish a single best approach for everyone.

    Percent or points? Reading a result slowly

    Illustration

    Studies often report a difference “relative to” a comparison group. A percentage of an average is not the same as percentage points. Choose any two numbers to see the difference. They are examples, not results from any study.

    10% lower than an average of 60 is 54: a gap of 6 points, not 10.

    What a single result cannot tell you, at any setting

    • Whether the same thing would happen with other learners, subjects, ages, or tools.
    • Whether the difference lasts, or how it compares with another way of teaching.
    • What any one learner in the study experienced.

      Dated findings

      Each line is Published estimate or a published description, inside that study’s own setting. Follow the link for design, sample, and limits.

      1. July 2023

        Seven AI-text detectors, tested in March 2023, labelled an average of 61% of 91 essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated, while classifying 88 US eighth-grade essays almost perfectly. Source note S29 →

      2. 5 May 2024

        In 11 randomized schools in Ghana, students using a WhatsApp maths tutor in supervised study halls grew 3.01 points more on a 35-item test than controls, who had no equal-time alternative. Source note S28 →

      3. 24 May 2024

        Among 274 adult crowdworkers, human-checked ChatGPT hints produced immediate algebra gains that were not significantly different from human-written hints. About a third of generated hints failed the check and were replaced. Source note S27 →

      4. 10 December 2024

        University students revising an essay with ChatGPT improved the essay more than those helped by a human expert or a checklist, but showed no difference in knowledge gain or transfer. Source note S26 →

      5. 15 January 2025

        26% of US teens said they had ever used ChatGPT for schoolwork, double the 2023 share. Most thought using it to research a topic was fine; few said the same of writing essays. Source note S31 →

      6. 8 March 2025

        In two lab experiments with novice programmers, chatbot access did not significantly change post-test learning. Exploratory analyses linked asking for explanations, rather than solutions, to better results. Source note S23 →

      7. 25 April 2025

        In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, higher confidence in AI for a task went with less self-reported critical thinking. The associations are not causal. Source note S25 →

      8. May 2025

        A six-week, teacher-supported after-school program in Benin City, Nigeria, reported a 0.31 standard-deviation gain on a combined English, AI-knowledge and digital-skills test. Materials, adults, and AI were bundled together, and attrition differed between groups. Source note S09 →

      9. 3 June 2025

        In one undergraduate physics course, students using a purpose-built AI tutor at home scored higher on an immediate post-test than after in-class active learning. The settings differed in more than the AI, and retention was not measured. Source note S08 →

      10. 24 June 2025

        Six in ten US public school teachers reported using AI for work. Weekly users estimated saving 5.9 hours a week; only 19% said their school had an AI policy. Source note S33 →

      11. 25 June 2025

        At one Turkish high school, a standard GPT-4 chat interface raised practice scores, but students who had it scored 17% lower than controls on a later unassisted exam. A guarded tutor version largely avoided that drop. Source note S01 →

      12. 31 December 2025

        An under-review MIT Media Lab preprint found weaker EEG connectivity, and poorer recall of their own essays, among 54 adults who wrote with ChatGPT. EEG is not a measure of learning, and the methods have been questioned. Source note S24 →

      13. 24 February 2026

        54% of US teens said they had used an AI chatbot for schoolwork help, and 59% believed students at their school use chatbots to cheat at least somewhat often. The question differs from Pew’s 2024 one. Source note S32 →

      14. Fielded March 2026

        Among US children aged 9 to 17 who used AI, broadly defined, 85% used it for schoolwork; 16% of homework users said they found it hard to start or finish homework without it. Source note S34 →

      15. June 2026

        Adding in-person engagement support to an AI reading platform for young children increased weekly use by about one to four minutes, from a low base. Reading outcomes were not significantly different. Source note S22 →

      16. 18 June 2026

        The OECD and European Commission published an AI literacy framework for primary and secondary education: understanding, critical evaluation, and ethical and creative use. It sets goals; it is not a trial. Source note S18 →

      17. 18 September 2026

        In a small Estonian study of 36 analysed students, a brief AI-literacy explanation did not significantly reduce adoption of incorrect AI advice, and rejection of correct advice rose. Source note S07 →

      18. October 2026

        In Lima, Peru, teachers offered training plus an AI assistant scored higher on prompt-writing rubrics and judged weak AI output better, with no significant time savings across seven self-reported tasks. Student learning was not measured as an outcome here. Source note S16 →

      What the research cannot say yet

      • How everyday AI use shapes learning and independent thinking over years, rather than weeks.
      • What happens when young children use general-purpose chatbots on their own.
      • Whether a short lesson about AI changes how people rely on it in ordinary use.
      • Which tutor designs and guardrails work across subjects, ages, languages, and countries.
      • Whether time a tool saves teachers turns into better learning for students, or how much time is really saved.
      • Whether brain-activity differences seen in short lab tasks mean anything for learning.

      Many studies here are single-site, short, or preprints, and several bundle AI with human support. Research method and open questions →

      Where it began / 09

      Where it began

      “Now I do believe PROPER use of these tools in education will be a great boon, and… inevitable.”

      — Spencer Hinson, in a Facebook post from August 13, 2023

      That early reflection is an origin marker for Dux: an invitation to keep asking how we can welcome useful tools while protecting the learning process itself. The post set out the tools of the moment, and then turned to what they would mean for schools, families, and the learners themselves.

      More from the post

      “There needs to be discussion around this because it will happen faster than you think and it already is.”
      “Because while the majority of the population may be unaware of these technologies, your kids may not be.”
      “There will need to be regulations in schools and parental guidance to fight all these tools so that kids don’t abuse them. So the flesh and blood kids still learn something. AI might be a great tool for us in the future… but over-reliance on it seems like a terrifying prospect and a recipe for disaster.”
      “So I hope just a little of all this I mentioned here, brings attention and awareness to the topic as you all head into the next school year… and the most interesting time for humanity there will ever be.”

      Read the original post, embedded alongside a verbatim copy →

      The post linked to a contemporaneous WBTV article on AI in the classroom ↗. Its discussion provides historical context for questions about school rules, parental guidance, genuine learning, and unreliable AI output.
